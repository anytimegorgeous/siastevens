What Happened to Us

A Fable of Modern Love
by Sia Stevens
Publisher Vessel & Ink Press (Seattle) Pages 339 pp. · ~74,000 words Format Paperback, first edition
ISBN 979-8-9936070-2-3 Category Fable / Relationships Structure 60 chapters in 12 parts

A jilted Seattle technologist spends a year reverse-engineering why love fades in this ambitious debut fable. Stevens opens on the genre’s quietest catastrophe: a Tuesday-night breakup with no villain, no affair, and no explanation beyond “the spark just faded”—and a parting dare from the departing boyfriend, Victor, to report back if she ever figures out why. Her narrator, who shares the author’s first name, takes the dare literally, addressing an email she cannot yet finish and pursuing its answer through a year of episodic encounters, each staged in a new landscape with a new teacher. A depleted doctoral student in a university library establishes connection as the human “superpower”; a newly wealthy college friend’s launch party exposes attraction as a response to perceived value; a near-disaster on Mount Rainier dramatizes exchange; a Thanksgiving homecoming argues that modern independence is “dependence in disguise,” rerouted from neighbors to paychecks. Later movements widen the lens—addiction on Seattle’s streets, a Hollywood marriage saved by the question “What have you two built together?,” a Himalayan monastery, a Greek village matriarch who rehabilitates gossip as women’s accountability infrastructure—before the threads converge in a public speech on cultivating “a million green flags.”

The machinery is unhidden: characters exist to deliver lessons, chapters close by restating their theses, and sages are never wrong. Readers requiring literary subtlety should look elsewhere; the subtitle is the warning label. But within the fable’s contract, Stevens executes unusually well. Her three-stage framework—attraction, reciprocation, co-creation—is coherent enough to survive scrutiny, and her central reframe, that boredom in love is a prompt to build rather than a verdict to obey, is genuinely fresh. The aphoristic prose overreaches at times (“the air itself paused to listen”) yet lands often enough to fill a commonplace book: “Depletion doesn’t flirt”; “Love that is only consumed burns down. Love that is built compounds.” The finale’s evolutionary sex-difference claims, though carefully hedged, will divide book clubs—by design, one suspects. Wisely, Stevens withholds the fairy-tale ending her narrator spends the book chasing, closing instead on conditions, labors, and an open door.

Readalikes

For readers of Paulo Coelho’s The Alchemist, Alain de Botton’s The Course of Love, and Mitch Albom—and for anyone who has stood in a doorway without being given a reason.

A talky, big-hearted parable that earns its sermons more often than not—modern-love self-help wearing the robes of story, and better for the disguise.
