Why Dating Feels So Much Harder Now

The people didn’t get worse. The conditions did. What happens to attraction when a brain built for scarcity gets handed an infinite pool, and why the most pursued generation in history feels so unchosen.

By Sia StevensNovember 6, 20266 min read

For nearly all of human history, your realistic dating pool was the people within walking distance. The school, the workplace, the village. Small pool, real scarcity. And scarcity did quiet work on the heart: the people available to you registered as valuable, because alternatives were few and hard to find.

Then, in a single decade, the pool became functionally infinite. Someone on a dating app now sees more attractive faces before breakfast than a medieval villager saw in a lifetime. Our brains were never designed for that input. They still fire for every face. They also tire, saturate, and recalibrate.

Value is relative, and always was

Researchers once asked married men how attractive their wives were. Then they showed them a series of photos of models and actresses, and asked again. The second answers came back lower. Nothing about the wives had changed in ten minutes. The context had.

We judge everything against the alternatives we can currently see. Which means anyone who controls what alternatives you see controls, to a frightening degree, what you can appreciate. Now hand that mechanism a smartphone.

Economics has an iron law for this: when the supply of anything explodes, its per-unit value collapses. True of gold, true of currency, and ruthlessly true of romantic value. Beauty, charm, wit, validation: every signal is now supplied at industrial scale by the feed, and every one is depreciating.

The attraction isn’t gone. It’s buried under inventory.

More choice, less choosing

The psychologist Sheena Iyengar once set up a jam-tasting table in a grocery store, some days with twenty-four flavors, some days with six. The big table drew the bigger crowd. But the six-jam table sold far more jam. More options produced more browsing and much less choosing.

You live the same paradox every night on a streaming app: infinite options, evening gone, nothing picked. In dating, it looks like shopping. Scroll, order, it looks perfect in the pictures, then it arrives, and: return, refund, repeat. And nobody invests in what they can send back.

The same market, seen from two seats

The machine hits everyone, just not identically, and seeing both sides helps people stop blaming each other for it.

Many men get the illusion of infinite access: a feed full of attainable-looking beauty and validation on demand, and then a month without a real conversation with a woman. Confidence inflated by what he sees, starved by what he lives.

Many women get the mirror image: an illusion of infinite interest. Two hundred likes and forty messages, by any historical measure the most pursued person who has ever lived. Then she reads them. Thirty are one word. Most were sent to a hundred women that evening. She isn’t drowning in commitment. She’s drowning in attention that costs nothing to send.

Options feed desire. Focus starves it.

Different symptoms, same disease: abundance of signal, scarcity of substance. (These are patterns across millions of people, not verdicts on any one person. Plenty of individuals live on the other side.)

Attraction tracks scarcity

Look at what has become rare: a date planned carefully, a remembered detail (“how did the interview go?”), effort still showing up in week six, someone who texts first, someone who plans the next date without being asked. Now look at what’s common: “hey wyd” at 11:47pm, the same-day cancellation, being one of four people they’re “talking to.”

The rare list reads like a list of attractive things, and the common one like a list of turn-offs. Whatever the machine mass-produces stops being magnetic. Whatever it can’t automate (kept plans, remembered details, reciprocated effort) becomes the new premium.

What to do about it

  • Run an exposure audit. For three days, count the minutes you spend browsing profiles or feeds of attractive strangers and curated couples. No judgment, just the number. Then ask what that exposure is doing to your baseline.
  • Cut one input for a week and notice what starts looking valuable again.
  • Close doors on purpose. In an abundance economy, focus is the new fidelity. Delete the app after you meet someone worth meeting twice. Stop browsing what you claim to have stopped shopping for.
  • Offer what can’t be automated. Plan the date. Remember the detail. Show up in week six.

Appreciation, in this market, is no longer a feeling that happens to you. It’s a discipline you run.
