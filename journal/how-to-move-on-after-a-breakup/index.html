How to Move On When “Nothing Happened”

No fight. No betrayal. No one else. Just “I don’t feel it anymore, and I don’t know why.” The breakups with no villain are the hardest to get over, and “just move on” is the worst advice for them.

By Sia StevensNovember 6, 20266 min read

It ended on a Tuesday, over takeout we didn’t finish. He said it the way people say these things now: quiet, rehearsed, apologetic. “I don’t feel it anymore. Nothing happened. There’s no one else. I just… don’t feel it.”

I had wanted a reason so badly I would have taken a lie. An affair. A flaw. A fight we never had. Anything with a shape to it. Instead I got a shrug in sentence form: nothing happened, and it ended anyway.

If that’s your breakup too, you already know why it’s so hard to get over. You can’t be angry at a villain who doesn’t exist. You can’t learn a lesson nobody can name. And everyone around you says the same three words.

Why “just move on” doesn’t work

If a project fails at work, there’s a post-mortem: a whiteboard, a team, a list of lessons. If a relationship fails, you’re told: shut up, move on, stop overthinking. And you drag your unanswered questions into the next relationship like broken glass in your pockets.

I debug code for a living. In all my years of bug reports, I have never been allowed to close one with nothing happened. Engineers have a name for this kind of failure: a silent failure. The system stops doing what it was built to do and never throws an error. No crash, no log, nothing to point at.

“Growing apart.” “Losing the spark.” “Falling out of love.” Every one of them is a shrug in nicer clothes. And the real danger of skipping the post-mortem isn’t the pain now. It’s repeating the same story with a different person and calling it a fresh start.

Why it physically hurts

Early love runs on the brain’s reward system, the same dopamine machinery that fires for food, winning, and drugs. So losing it behaves like withdrawal: the craving, the intrusive thoughts, the 2am urge to text. Heartbreak is the one addiction you’re expected to detox in public. At your desk. Smiling.

That’s worth knowing for one reason: if you have ever white-knuckled a no-contact rule, you were not weak. You were detoxing. Treat it like that. It passes, and it passes faster when you stop feeding it.

Write your own post-mortem

You don’t need a reason from them to get an explanation for yourself. You need the right questions.

  • Write the story in three sentences. Exactly the way you’d have told it the month it ended. Keep it somewhere you can find. Later you’ll rewrite those three sentences with a better vocabulary, and the distance between the two versions is what you learned.
  • Find when the attention stopped. Not when it ended; when it started fading. When did the questions stop? When did their eyes drift to the phone mid-sentence? In my story, the attention was abundant at the start and quietly slipped away long before the Tuesday.
  • Name the stage that stalled. “The spark faded” usually means attraction stalled. “I felt like the only one trying” means the exchange went one-way. “We were just going with the flow” means nothing was being built anymore. Each points to something different, and something you can do differently next time.
  • Look at your move at the drop. When the intensity faded, what did you do: leave, panic, go quiet, or start building? Most of us have one signature move.

Closure is something you give yourself

In What Happened to Us, Sia drafts an email to the man who left, with the subject line What happened to us, and refuses to send it until she can honestly answer it. It takes her a year. When she finally sends it, she realizes she never wrote it for his answer. She wrote it for hers.

You never have to send yours. But if you wrote it, to whoever left you with nothing, what would the first line say?
