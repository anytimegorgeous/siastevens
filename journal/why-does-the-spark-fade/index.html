Why Does the Spark Fade? It’s Not the End of Love

“I love them, but I’m not in love anymore.” Nothing failed. The flood receded on schedule, because floods do. Here is what is actually happening, and the one move guaranteed to make it worse.

By Sia StevensNovember 6, 20266 min read

Somewhere between six and eighteen months in, it happens to almost every couple. “I’ve never felt anything like this” quietly becomes “I love them, but I’m not in love anymore.”

A face that once hit like a drug becomes, over the months, a glass of wine. Then a sip. Then water. You used to text every waking minute. Now it’s twice a day, and even that is mostly logistics wearing the costume of conversation.

And here is the fact that decides everything: nobody in that story changed. Same face. Same voice. Same two people. Something else changed, right on schedule.

Early love is a drug. That’s not a metaphor.

The anthropologist Helen Fisher spent her career putting newly-in-love people into brain scanners. What lit up wasn’t primarily the emotion centers. It was the reward system: the ancient dopamine machinery that fires for food, for winning, and for cocaine. Love, she concluded, behaves less like an emotion and more like a drive, as basic as hunger.

That explains why new love makes intelligent people briefly insane. It also explains why losing it feels exactly like withdrawal: the craving, the intrusive thoughts, the 2am urge to text. If you have ever white-knuckled a no-contact rule, you were not weak. You were detoxing.

The system has one non-negotiable feature: tolerance

The brain does not sustain a flood. Whatever the stimulus, whether sugar, a win, or a face, repetition dulls the response. The first bite of chocolate cake is transcendent. The fortieth consecutive daily slice is furniture. Nothing is wrong with the cake. The receiver adjusted.

This isn’t a defect that good relationships avoid. It is the operating system, and it comes for every couple that has ever lived. Researchers who tracked lottery winners and people who had been seriously injured found both groups drifted back near their old baseline of happiness within a year. The brain resets to everything: jackpots, tragedies, and yes, people.

The fade of intensity is not the end of love. It’s the end of the advertisement for love.

Why “the spark died” is the most predictable sentence in the language

Nobody tells us about tolerance, so an entire dating culture misreads the receding as a verdict. Most of us know a spark-chaser, or have been one: serial relationships, each abandoned at the intensity drop, each exit justified the same way. The feeling faded, so it must not have been right.

They aren’t exactly commitment-phobic. They’re chasing a chemical state no single human can supply indefinitely, mistaking the drug for the person. Every restart delivers the flood again, briefly, which seems to confirm the theory and resets the clock. Still fluent in beginnings. Still illiterate in everything after.

The quieter version is the couple who did nothing wrong and drifted anyway, waiting for the old feeling to return. It doesn’t return on its own.

What the flood was actually for

Evolutionarily speaking, the early rush exists to get two strangers over the wall of unfamiliarity and bond them fast. What comes after it isn’t less. It’s different, and it runs on a different fuel.

The early state is handed to you free, unearned, by chemistry. The lasting state is built. That sounds like bad news until you notice the liberation inside it: you have no control over a flood. You have enormous control over a build.

The brain wants two things at once: the familiar and the new. A relationship feeds the familiar automatically, every day, through shared mornings and private jokes. The only appetite a couple has to work on is the pull toward the new. And the variety that pull goes looking for outside the relationship can be found inside it.

What to do when the intensity drops

When the intensity drops in a relationship that is otherwise good (and it will), don’t read it as love dying. Read it as the free trial ending. What you do next decides everything, and “wait for the old feeling” is the one move guaranteed to fail.

  • Name your move. Chart your last two or three significant relationships: the surge, the peak, and the exact point the intensity dropped. Then write what you did there: left, checked out, panicked, coasted, or started building. Most people find one signature move repeated everywhere, and have been calling its results “my luck with love.”
  • Introduce one genuinely new experience this month. Not a nicer version of your usual dinner. Something neither of you has done before.
  • Build something together. A ritual, a project, a tradition. New creation is, by definition, novel, and the brain responds to it the way it responds to any new thing.

Shakespeare put the whole lesson in the mouth of a friar four centuries ago: “These violent delights have violent ends… therefore love moderately; long love doth so.” He wasn’t lowering the ceiling. He was describing the door.
