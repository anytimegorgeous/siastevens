Breadcrumbing, Situationships and the Chill Girl: Why Low Effort Became Normal

Why does nobody put in effort anymore? Why do you get called demanding for asking for the bare minimum? The answer isn’t that people got worse. It’s what the modern world delivers, and what it can’t.

By Sia StevensNovember 6, 20267 min read

Third cancellation in a row

Hey sorry, I can’t make it tonight. Something came up.

No worries! Another time 😊

That’s three times now. If you want to see me, you’ll need to be the one to reschedule.

Two couples, sixty years apart

Start in 1962, with Don and Marie. Look at what Don wants: a hot meal at the end of the day, someone to come home to, to be admired, and the sight of a woman’s body. Every one of those routes through Marie. There is no other supplier. If he wants any of it, he has to be chosen, and then stay chosen.

Marie’s list is a family, a future, safety across the years, somebody who will still be there. Every item routes through Don. Two lists, one supplier each, so both of them had to be worth choosing, and both of them knew it.

Now meet Jason and Claire, today. The hot meal is twenty minutes away on an app. Admiration arrives on a screen, from strangers, all day. The sight of a woman’s body is free and unlimited. Someone new is always a swipe away. Every appetite on Don’s list is delivered to Jason’s door, and not one requires a woman to say yes.

Claire’s list hasn’t changed at all. A family. A future. Somebody who stays. And nobody has built that app, because there is no service that produces a person who shows up, day after day, on ordinary days when nothing is forcing them to.

The market filled one side’s list and left the other exactly where it was.

That gap is the imbalance, and it’s nobody’s fault. It doesn’t make men villains, either. Follow Jason forward to forty-five: good job, every app, food and company within the hour, and on a Sunday evening, scrolling on the couch, feeling nothing at all. His appetites got fed every day. His needs never did. Nobody won.

Why it lands as breadcrumbing

When something is abundant, people stop fighting to keep it. Think about a paper cup: you drink from it, crush it, toss it. You’d never do that to a ceramic mug you love. And why would anyone apologize to a paper cup?

That’s the low-effort dating we all recognize. The 11:47pm “hey wyd.” The date cancelled the day of. The situationship with no title and no milestones. One person initiates, compromises and holds the whole thing together. The other contributes the bare minimum and sincerely believes it’s enough.

The chill girl subsidy

The most common response is to make yourself easier to keep. In What Happened to Us, Rhea spends fifteen years as the chill girl. The girl who says “it’s fine.” Who texts back “no worries!” Who swallows her questions because chill girls don’t ask. Every time he drifts, she resets the score and calls it a fresh start: the first chance, repeated on a loop, wearing a different date.

Years later, she finally does the math on all those free second chances:

“I wasn’t forgiving them. I was funding them.”

Unlimited second chances don’t outlast bad behavior. They pay for it. People don’t treat you the way you say you want to be treated. They treat you the way you’ve shown them you’ll put up with.

How to hold a line without starting a war

A boundary isn’t a punishment, and it isn’t a fortress. It’s information: what earns your investment and what forfeits it. Three things make it work, and none of them is volume.

  • State it in peace. Lines invented mid-crisis read as tantrums. Lines stated calmly and early read as terms.
  • Make the consequence something you stop doing, never something you do to them. Your investment pauses; your availability shrinks to match theirs. And keep it at low temperature. Anger reads as weather that will pass. Calm reads as fact.
  • Know the difference between an accident and a pattern. Once is an accident, and accidents get let go. Every time is a pattern, and patterns get a consequence.

So when he cancels for the third time in a row, “No worries! Another time” rewards the pattern. “That’s three times now. If you want to see me, you’ll need to be the one to reschedule” holds the line, calmly, without a fight.

A line is never only yours

Every held line tells the people watching what can be asked for. Every dropped one shows the next person the real price. The bar didn’t fall in one dramatic moment. It fell one accepted crumb at a time. Holding your line raises the floor for everyone near you.

The fix for both sides is the same: strengthen the connection. When two people genuinely need each other and build a shared life, the counting stops. Scorekeeping isn’t what breaks a relationship. It’s what shows up when the joint account was never opened.
